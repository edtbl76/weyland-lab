# Affinity Mapping (Affinity Diagramming)

## What It Is
A bottom-up synthesis technique developed by Jiro Kawakita (also known as the KJ Method) that organizes a large number of observations, ideas, or data points into meaningful clusters by natural affinity — allowing patterns and themes to emerge from the data rather than being imposed on it. Participants write individual observations on sticky notes (one observation per note), then collaboratively move notes into groups that feel related, and label each group with a header that captures the theme. Affinity mapping transforms raw, unorganized research data into structured insight and is one of the most frequently used facilitation techniques in design, product, and research synthesis.

## When to Use
- After user research or contextual inquiry: synthesizing observations into themes
- When a team has large amounts of unorganized data (interview notes, survey responses, workshop outputs)
- Converting a brainstorm's raw outputs into structured categories
- At the start of a Design Sprint: organizing research outputs before problem framing
- Any workshop where a team needs to move from divergent outputs to convergent themes

## Key Concepts
- **One Observation Per Note**: The cardinal rule. Notes that combine multiple observations cannot be sorted accurately. "User was confused by navigation and also had trouble finding support" → two separate notes
- **Silent Sorting**: In the initial sorting phase, participants move notes silently — without verbal negotiation. This allows organic groupings to emerge without influence from the most vocal participants. Discuss only when notes are contested or groups need headers
- **Emergent Themes**: Affinity mapping works bottom-up: the themes are named after the clusters form, not before. Top-down categorization (assigning notes to pre-existing categories) is not affinity mapping — it's sorting, which confirms hypotheses rather than revealing them
- **Hierarchy**: Affinity maps can have multiple levels — observation notes cluster into themes, themes cluster into super-themes. Two levels is usually sufficient; three levels is appropriate for large data sets
- **Header Quality**: The header of each cluster should capture the insight, not just the topic. "Users are frustrated with navigation" (insight) is better than "Navigation" (topic). Insight headers produce better HMW questions and design direction
- **Digital vs. Physical**: Physical affinity mapping on a wall with sticky notes is more engaging and produces better group synthesis than digital tools. When in-person is impossible, FigJam, Miro, and Mural support digital affinity mapping, but require more facilitation to maintain engagement
- **Affinity Map as Deliverable**: The affinity map can be photographed and documented as a research deliverable — the clusters become the findings, the headers become the key themes
- **Relationship to KJ Method**: The original KJ Method includes additional steps after clustering: drawing relationship arrows between groups, prioritizing groups. In consulting practice, these steps are often replaced by dot voting on the clusters

## Method Application
Method uses affinity mapping as the standard research synthesis technique after user interviews, contextual inquiry sessions, or workshop divergence phases. The map converts raw qualitative data into structured themes that drive HMW question generation, journey map validation, and requirements prioritization. It is also used to synthesize stakeholder feedback after workshops to identify common themes without attribution.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Affinity Mapping**: The most important facilitation rule for affinity mapping is protecting the silent sorting phase. When participants talk while sorting, the most senior or most vocal person's interpretation shapes the clusters — and the emergent discovery benefit is lost. Start with 10-15 minutes of silent sorting, then allow discussion only for headers and contested notes.
