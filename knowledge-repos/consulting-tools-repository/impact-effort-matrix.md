# Impact-Effort Matrix (2×2 Prioritization)

## What It Is
A visual prioritization tool that positions ideas, features, or initiatives on a 2×2 grid with Impact (high/low) on the vertical axis and Effort (high/low) on the horizontal axis — producing four quadrants that reveal where to invest, what to defer, what to do quickly, and what to avoid. The four quadrant labels: Quick Wins (high impact, low effort — do first), Major Projects (high impact, high effort — plan and invest), Fill-Ins (low impact, low effort — do when capacity allows), and Time Sinks (low impact, high effort — avoid). The matrix is a rapid group alignment tool — more useful for creating shared perspective and sparking discussion than for producing precise rankings.

## When to Use
- Workshop prioritization: quickly sorting a large set of ideas after a brainstorm
- Backlog grooming: identifying which backlog items to pull in vs. defer
- After dot voting: adding effort dimension to the items that surfaced as high interest
- When a team needs to make fast relative prioritization decisions without detailed scoring
- Communicating investment rationale to stakeholders in accessible, visual terms

## Key Concepts
- **Four Quadrants**:
  - *Quick Wins (High Impact, Low Effort)*: The highest priority — maximum return with minimum investment. Do these immediately. Common in discovery: low-hanging fruit that stakeholders haven't noticed
  - *Major Projects (High Impact, High Effort)*: Important but require planning and sustained investment. These belong on the roadmap with proper scoping
  - *Fill-Ins (Low Impact, Low Effort)*: Do when capacity exists. Not a priority but not waste; often maintenance, minor improvements, or polish
  - *Time Sinks (Low Impact, High Effort)*: Avoid. These consume resources without proportional return. The matrix's value often lies in making time sinks visible — items that feel important because they're complex, not because they deliver value
- **Relative Positioning**: Items on the matrix are positioned relative to each other, not against an absolute scale. An item in the Quick Wins quadrant means it is high impact and low effort relative to the other items being evaluated — not in absolute terms
- **Facilitation Process**: Write each item on a sticky note; place the first item in the center; position all subsequent items relative to the first. When items cluster, ask "of these, which is higher impact?" to spread them. Simultaneous sorting produces faster results than sequential voting
- **Impact Definition**: "Impact" must be defined before the exercise begins — impact on what? User adoption? Revenue? Team velocity? Without a shared definition, different participants are positioning items against different criteria
- **Effort Definition**: "Effort" typically means development effort, but can include organizational effort, cost, or risk. Clarify with the group
- **Not a Replacement for Detailed Estimation**: The impact-effort matrix is a fast relative tool. Before committing to a "Quick Win," validate that the effort estimate holds up under detailed planning. Many quick wins turn into major projects upon closer inspection

## Method Application
Method uses the impact-effort matrix as a fast prioritization tool in requirements workshops, backlog grooming sessions, and roadmap planning. It provides a visual snapshot that stakeholders can react to — "this item is here?" — triggering discussion about misaligned assumptions. The matrix result feeds into MoSCoW prioritization or RICE scoring for more precise ranking of the highest-priority items.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Impact-Effort Matrix**: The most productive workshop moment from an impact-effort matrix is when stakeholders discover that something they considered high-impact is in the Time Sinks quadrant. "We've been planning to spend a month on this — and the team placed it as low impact relative to everything else?" That misalignment surfaces assumptions that were never made explicit.
