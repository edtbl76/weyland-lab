# Design Sprint

## What It Is
A five-day structured problem-solving process developed at Google Ventures (GV) by Jake Knapp that compresses months of design and validation work into a single week. The Design Sprint follows a defined daily structure: Monday (Map and understand), Tuesday (Sketch solutions), Wednesday (Decide), Thursday (Prototype), Friday (Test with real users). At the end of five days, the team has tested a realistic prototype with five target customers — producing validated learning without building anything production-ready. The sprint answers a critical question before committing to development: "Will this solution work for our users?"

## When to Use
- Before committing to a major product investment where the right solution is uncertain
- When a team is stuck in analysis paralysis or stakeholder disagreement about the right approach
- Discovery phase kickoff: rapidly align on direction before requirements analysis begins
- Evaluating two or more competing solution approaches in one week
- New product concepts, new features with significant UX complexity, or significant pivots

## Key Concepts
- **Day 1 — Map**: Define the long-term goal, map the user journey, identify the critical moment to focus on. The sprint question is established: "What do we want to learn by Friday?" Teams that skip this day often sprint toward the wrong target
- **Day 2 — Sketch**: Individual solution sketching (not group brainstorming). Each participant produces a four-panel storyboard independently. Divergent thinking before group influence. Techniques: Lightning Demos, Crazy 8s, Solution Sketch
- **Day 3 — Decide**: Silent review of all sketches, heat-map voting, structured critique, and final decision. One solution is chosen for prototyping — not a composite of everyone's ideas. Decision by the Decider, not consensus
- **Day 4 — Prototype**: Build a realistic facade — not a functional product, but realistic enough that users respond as if it were real. Tool-agnostic: Keynote, Figma, a physical mock-up, a role-played service. Prototype only what will be tested
- **Day 5 — Test**: Five user interviews following a structured script. Patterns typically emerge by the third interview. The team observes together — not debrief later. Output: validated or invalidated hypotheses
- **The Decider**: Sprints require one person with final decision authority who participates fully. Without a Decider, Wednesday's decision step stalls. This is the most common sprint failure mode
- **Sprint vs. Design Thinking**: Design sprints operationalize design thinking principles into a concrete time-boxed process. Design thinking is the philosophy; design sprints are the practice
- **Sprint as a Communication Tool**: Even when the prototype test is inconclusive, the sprint creates shared understanding among stakeholders that is impossible to achieve through documentation alone. "We all watched the same five users respond" is more convincing than any report

## Method Application
Method runs Design Sprints at the start of product engagements where the solution space is uncertain. The sprint de-risks the delivery commitment: if the prototype test reveals the concept is wrong, it's better to know in week 1 than after six months of development. Also used when stakeholder alignment on direction is blocking the program — a sprint creates shared experience rather than shared documents.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Design Sprint**: The most powerful moment in a Design Sprint is the client watching real users struggle with the prototype on Friday. No stakeholder presentation, no requirements document, and no prototype review produces the same outcome as five minutes of watching a user say "I don't understand what I'm supposed to do here." This is why the sprint is worth running even when the outcome seems obvious — what's obvious to the team is often opaque to users. → `consulting-tools-repository/design-sprint.md`
