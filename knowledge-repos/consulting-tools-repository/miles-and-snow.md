# Miles and Snow Framework

## What It Is
A typology of competitive strategies developed by Raymond Miles and Charles Snow that classifies organizations into four strategic archetypes based on how they respond to market change: Prospectors (constantly seek new opportunities, innovate first), Defenders (protect narrow, stable domains), Analyzers (combine Prospector and Defender — innovate in some areas, defend in others), and Reactors (no consistent strategy; respond only when forced). Unlike Porter's generic strategies which describe competitive positioning, Miles and Snow describes the organizational DNA and operating model required to sustain each strategic orientation.

## When to Use
- Organizational alignment conversations: the strategy and operating model must be congruent (a Prospector operating like a Defender will fail)
- Digital transformation engagements: is the client trying to become a Prospector but organized as a Defender?
- M&A integration: merging a Prospector culture with a Defender creates dysfunction
- Technology strategy: Prospectors need fast experimentation infrastructure; Defenders need reliable, efficient platforms
- When a client's strategy and execution patterns are misaligned — they claim to be innovative but act defensively

## Key Concepts
- **Prospector**: Continuously seeks product and market innovation. Values flexibility and adaptability over efficiency. First to market; willing to sacrifice short-term profit for long-term positioning. Requires: loosely structured organization, decentralized decision-making, tolerance for failure, investment in R&D. Technology profile: high innovation velocity, experimentation platform, willingness to accumulate technical debt
- **Defender**: Operates in a narrow, stable product/market domain. Competes on efficiency, reliability, and deep expertise. Rarely pursues innovation outside the core. Requires: tight process control, functional specialization, cost optimization, strong operational metrics. Technology profile: stability-focused, mature systems, cost-efficient infrastructure, high reliability
- **Analyzer**: Occupies the middle — maintains a stable core business while cautiously entering new product/market areas that Prospectors have validated. Watches competitors, enters with lower risk. Requires: matrix organization, both R&D and operational efficiency. Technology profile: stable core systems alongside innovation labs; fast-follow capability
- **Reactor**: Lacks a consistent strategy; changes direction based on environmental pressure. Characterized by strategic incoherence — strategy changes before the organization adapts. Symptom: the organization's processes, structure, and technology don't fit any consistent strategic logic. Technology profile: fragmented, inconsistent, reactive investment patterns
- **Congruence Requirement**: The critical insight is that each archetype requires a congruent organizational design — strategy, structure, processes, and technology must all align. A Prospector that tries to operate with a Defender's governance model creates strategic dysfunction
- **No "Best" Archetype**: Each is valid in the right context. Defenders win in stable, efficiency-driven industries; Prospectors win in dynamic, high-growth markets; Analyzers balance both. Reactors are always underperforming

## Method Application
Used in organizational transformation engagements to diagnose misalignment between stated strategy and actual operating model. A client claiming to be "digital-first and innovative" (Prospector aspiration) but with centralized governance, risk-averse culture, and annual planning cycles (Defender reality) has a misalignment problem. The framework gives language to the gap and structures the transformation agenda.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Miles and Snow**: The most powerful use of Miles and Snow is diagnosing the gap between strategic aspiration and organizational reality. Most digital transformation clients aspire to Prospector behavior but are organized and governed as Defenders — the transformation program must address the operating model, not just the technology. Ask: "What archetype does your governance model, budget cycle, and risk tolerance imply?" Then compare it to stated strategy.
