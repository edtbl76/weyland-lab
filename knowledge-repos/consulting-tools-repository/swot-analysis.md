# SWOT Analysis

## What It Is
A structured strategic planning tool that evaluates an organization or initiative across four dimensions: Strengths (internal capabilities that provide competitive advantage), Weaknesses (internal limitations that hinder performance), Opportunities (external conditions that could be exploited), and Threats (external conditions that could cause harm). SWOT is the most widely used strategy framework in business — familiar to executives, easy to facilitate, and effective as a starting point for strategic conversation. Its value is not in the 2×2 grid but in the SO/ST/WO/WT strategies it generates: how do you use Strengths to pursue Opportunities? How do you use Strengths to counter Threats?

## When to Use
- Kickoff facilitation when the client team needs a shared baseline of strategic context
- Pre-engagement research to identify where Method can add the most value
- When a client needs to justify a strategic decision to their board or leadership team
- As a synthesis tool after PESTLE and Five Forces analysis — those inform the Opportunities and Threats
- Program-level assessments: "What are this program's strengths and risks?"

## Key Concepts
- **Internal vs. External**: Strengths and Weaknesses are internal (within the organization's control). Opportunities and Threats are external (in the environment, not controllable). This distinction matters: a declining market is a Threat, not a Weakness
- **SO Strategies (Maxi-Maxi)**: Use Strengths to exploit Opportunities. The most offensive strategy — where the organization should invest to grow
- **ST Strategies (Maxi-Mini)**: Use Strengths to mitigate Threats. Defensive use of core capabilities
- **WO Strategies (Mini-Maxi)**: Address Weaknesses to pursue Opportunities. Investment/capability-building agenda
- **WT Strategies (Mini-Mini)**: Minimize Weaknesses and avoid Threats. Defensive, risk-reduction posture
- **Common Failure Mode**: SWOTs filled with vague platitudes ("strong brand," "competitive market") that don't generate actionable strategies. Push for specificity: not "strong engineering team" but "proprietary ML recommendation engine with 3 years of training data that competitors cannot replicate in under 18 months"
- **Prioritization**: Not all quadrant items are equal. Ask: which 2-3 Strengths are truly differentiating? Which 1-2 Threats are existential? Focus the conversation on the highest-weight items
- **TOWS Matrix**: The TOWS Matrix (Threats-Opportunities-Weaknesses-Strengths) explicitly generates the SO/ST/WO/WT strategies — a useful facilitation step after the SWOT is populated

## Method Application
Used in strategy workshops as a shared baseline before diving into specific workstreams. Most valuable when facilitated with mixed stakeholder groups — engineering, product, and business leaders will identify different Strengths and Threats, and the divergence is informative. Follow the SWOT with TOWS strategy generation to prevent the common failure of a SWOT that generates no decisions.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — SWOT Analysis**: A SWOT without a TOWS is a list, not a strategy. After populating the four quadrants, always run the SO/WO/ST/WT strategy generation — "given these Strengths, which Opportunities are we best positioned to pursue?" This step converts the familiar 2×2 into strategic direction. Push for specificity in every box: vague SWOTs produce vague strategies.
