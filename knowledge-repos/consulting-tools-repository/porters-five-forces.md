# Porter's Five Forces

## What It Is
A competitive analysis framework developed by Michael Porter that structures the competitive intensity and attractiveness of an industry across five forces: Competitive Rivalry (intensity of competition among existing players), Threat of New Entrants (barriers to entry), Threat of Substitutes (alternative ways to meet customer needs), Bargaining Power of Buyers (customer leverage), and Bargaining Power of Suppliers (vendor/partner leverage). The combined strength of these forces determines industry profitability potential and informs where a firm should position itself to achieve sustainable competitive advantage.

## When to Use
- Pre-engagement research to understand a client's industry competitive dynamics
- Strategy engagements where the client needs to understand why margins are under pressure
- Technology investment justification: "which force does this investment neutralize?"
- M&A due diligence: assessing the competitive position of an acquisition target
- When a client faces a disruptive new entrant or substitute and needs a response strategy

## Key Concepts
- **Competitive Rivalry**: High rivalry compresses margins. Drivers: number of competitors, industry growth rate, product differentiation, exit barriers. Technology can reduce rivalry by differentiating (unique platform) or increase it (when technology lowers barriers to clone)
- **Threat of New Entrants**: High threat limits profitability. Barriers to entry: capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty, switching costs, regulatory requirements, access to distribution. Technology often lowers barriers (SaaS vs. enterprise software), increasing this threat
- **Threat of Substitutes**: Substitutes constrain pricing. Consider non-obvious substitutes — Zoom substituted for business travel, not just competing video platforms. Digital products frequently face substitution risk from platforms (e.g., apps displaced by OS-native features)
- **Bargaining Power of Buyers**: Concentrated buyers with low switching costs have high bargaining power. Enterprise technology with 3 major customers: those customers control the roadmap. Platform thinking can reduce this by serving many buyers
- **Bargaining Power of Suppliers**: Concentrated, differentiated suppliers hold power. Cloud concentration (AWS/Azure/GCP) is a supplier power issue. Open source reduces supplier power; proprietary dependencies increase it
- **Sixth Force — Complementors**: Sometimes added (Andy Grove): parties whose products or services complement yours, increasing your value. Platform ecosystems leverage complementors strategically

## Method Application
Used in pre-engagement research and solutions strategy to frame technology investment in competitive terms. Helps answer: "why does this technology investment matter strategically?" A client investing in a customer data platform because buyers are gaining power (they can switch easily) has a clearer strategic rationale than one doing it for operational efficiency alone.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Porter's Five Forces**: Use Five Forces to anchor technology investment in business strategy — it transforms "we need a better platform" into "our switching costs are low and buyers have high power; we need to raise the cost of leaving." The most useful force in technology consulting is usually Threat of New Entrants (has technology lowered barriers?) and Threat of Substitutes (is a platform or adjacent product making this category irrelevant?).
