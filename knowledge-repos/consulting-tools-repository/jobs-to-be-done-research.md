# Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) Research

## What It Is
A theoretical framework and research methodology, developed by Clayton Christensen and Tony Ulwick, that reframes what customers want from product features to the underlying jobs they are trying to accomplish. The core insight: customers don't buy products — they hire them to get a job done. The job is stable even as the solutions change: the job of "communicate with friends at a distance" has been addressed by letters, telegrams, phones, email, SMS, and social media. Understanding the job — not the current solution — reveals enduring requirements and enables innovation. JTBD research uses specific interview techniques to surface the causal mechanisms behind purchase and usage decisions.

## When to Use
- Discovery research for new products or major feature investments
- When a client's product is losing adoption and the team doesn't understand why
- When requirements conversations keep circling back to "more features" without customer evidence
- Reframing stakeholder-driven feature requests into customer-need language
- Innovation programs: identifying new solution spaces for existing jobs

## Key Concepts
- **The Job Statement**: Structured as "When [situation], I want to [motivation/goal], so I can [expected outcome]." The situation is the trigger; the motivation is the job; the outcome is the success criterion. Jobs are functional, emotional, and social
- **The Hire/Fire Metaphor**: Customers "hire" a product when they need a job done; they "fire" it when it does the job poorly or a better option appears. Churn analysis through a JTBD lens reveals what job the product failed to do, not just that customers left
- **Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI)**: Tony Ulwick's application of JTBD — customers evaluate solutions by how well they help execute each step of the job. ODI maps the job as a process (job steps) and identifies which steps are underserved (high importance, low satisfaction). The underserved steps are the innovation opportunity
- **JTBD Interviews**: Structured chronological interviews that walk customers through a purchase or switching decision: what was happening before you started looking for a solution? What did you try first? What made you choose this solution? What were you hoping for? The interview surface the situational context, anxiety, and momentum that drove the decision
- **Progress vs. Features**: JTBD frames customer value as progress — moving from a current struggling state to a desired outcome. Features are mechanisms of progress; the customer cares about the destination, not the mechanism
- **Job Executor vs. Buyer vs. End User**: In B2B contexts, the person who approves the budget (buyer) often has different jobs than the person who uses the product (job executor). JTBD research must address both
- **Switch Interview**: A specific JTBD interview technique focused on the moment of switch — when a customer moved from an old solution to a new one. The switch interview reveals the four forces: push (pain with current solution), pull (attraction to new solution), anxiety (fear of switching), inertia (habits preventing switch)

## Method Application
Method uses JTBD research in discovery phases to reframe requirements from a list of features the client wants to build into a map of jobs customers are trying to get done. This reframe produces better-prioritized requirements and a more defensible business case for investment — "we're investing in this because it addresses an underserved job with high customer importance" is a stronger justification than "our product manager thinks users want this."

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Jobs to Be Done Research**: The most powerful JTBD intervention is running a single well-structured switch interview with a recently acquired customer and playing the recording to the product team. When customers describe in their own words what they were struggling with, what they tried, and why they finally chose this solution — the product team's feature backlog reorganizes itself.
