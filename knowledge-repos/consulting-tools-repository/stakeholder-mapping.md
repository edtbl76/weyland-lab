# Stakeholder Mapping

## What It Is
A systematic process for identifying, analyzing, and planning engagement with the people and groups who have an interest in, influence over, or are affected by a program or initiative. Stakeholder mapping produces a visual or structured artifact that positions stakeholders by their level of influence and level of interest (or support/opposition) — enabling targeted engagement strategies for each group. In consulting and technology programs, stakeholder mapping is a prerequisite for change management, requirements gathering, and program governance — you cannot manage what you haven't mapped.

## When to Use
- Program kickoff: identifying who must be engaged, informed, or managed before work begins
- When a program is experiencing resistance from unexpected sources
- Requirements analysis: who are the right people to interview for requirements, and in what order?
- Change planning: who needs to be in the Guiding Coalition? Who needs ADKAR intervention?
- Executive communication planning: who is the audience for which program update?

## Key Concepts
- **Influence/Interest Matrix**: The most common stakeholder mapping tool — a 2×2 matrix with Influence (high/low) on one axis and Interest (high/low) on the other. Four quadrants:
  - High Influence / High Interest: Manage Closely — primary engagement targets
  - High Influence / Low Interest: Keep Satisfied — don't ignore; they can block you
  - Low Influence / High Interest: Keep Informed — they are advocates; inform them regularly
  - Low Influence / Low Interest: Monitor — minimal engagement; don't waste capacity
- **Support/Opposition Analysis**: Overlaying stakeholder positions on the influence/interest map — who is a champion, who is neutral, who is resistant? High-influence resisters are the primary risk; low-influence champions can be mobilized to help with outreach
- **Stakeholder Onion Diagram**: An alternative visualization — concentric rings from the program center outward, with stakeholders positioned by proximity to the program. Useful for showing dependency vs. interest relationships
- **Power Mapping**: In politically complex programs, mapping informal power (who do people actually listen to?) alongside formal authority reveals influence networks that org charts don't show
- **Dynamic Nature**: Stakeholder positions shift throughout a program. Champions become resisters when scope changes affect their team; neutral stakeholders become advocates after a successful demo. The map requires maintenance, not just initial creation
- **Stakeholder Register**: A structured artifact (table or database) capturing each stakeholder's role, influence, interest, current position, engagement strategy, and key concerns. Typically maintained by the Program Management Office
- **RACI Relationship**: Stakeholder mapping identifies who exists in the ecosystem; RACI assigns their roles for specific activities and decisions. A stakeholder can be influential in the map but "I" (Informed) in the RACI

## Method Application
Method builds a stakeholder map within the first week of every engagement. The map shapes who is included in requirements sessions, who is presented to for program reviews, and who needs dedicated change management investment. Failing to map stakeholders early results in surprises — a senior leader who becomes a blocker because they were never engaged, or a user community that resists adoption because they were never consulted.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Stakeholder Mapping**: The most common stakeholder mapping failure is treating it as a one-time administrative exercise rather than a living intelligence product. Build the initial map in week one, then update it after every significant program event — a demo, a scope change, a leadership review. The map should tell you, at any point in the program, where your risks and your advocates are. If the map hasn't changed in two months, it hasn't been maintained. → `consulting-tools-repository/stakeholder-mapping.md`
