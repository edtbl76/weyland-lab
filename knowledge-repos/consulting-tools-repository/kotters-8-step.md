# Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

## What It Is
An organizational change management framework developed by John Kotter (Harvard Business School) based on analysis of why large-scale organizational change efforts succeed or fail. The model defines eight sequential steps for leading transformation: (1) Create a Sense of Urgency, (2) Build a Guiding Coalition, (3) Form a Strategic Vision and Initiatives, (4) Enlist a Volunteer Army, (5) Enable Action by Removing Barriers, (6) Generate Short-Term Wins, (7) Sustain Acceleration, and (8) Institute Change. Originally published as an 8-step process, Kotter later updated the model to reflect a dual-operating system where the hierarchical organization and a network-based change coalition operate in parallel. The model is the most widely referenced organizational change framework in management literature.

## When to Use
- Planning and leading large-scale organizational change in client engagements
- When technology transformation is stalling due to organizational resistance
- Diagnosing which step a change effort is stuck on
- Structuring a change leadership plan for executive sponsors
- When clients need a common vocabulary for talking about change management with their leadership team

## Key Concepts
- **Step 1 — Sense of Urgency**: Change efforts fail when leaders underestimate inertia. Creating urgency requires communicating the consequence of not changing — not as abstract risk, but as vivid near-term threat or opportunity. "The burning platform"
- **Step 2 — Guiding Coalition**: A diverse, credible group with the authority, expertise, and relationships to lead change. Not just senior leadership — must include informal influencers who have organizational trust at multiple levels
- **Step 3 — Vision and Strategy**: A clear, compelling picture of what the future state looks like and why it matters. Vision that cannot be communicated in five minutes is too complex. Strategy without vision is a list of activities
- **Step 4 — Communicate the Vision**: Most organizations under-communicate by a factor of ten. Vision must be embedded in everyday decisions, meetings, and behaviors — not just announced in a kick-off
- **Step 5 — Remove Barriers**: Structural, process, and cultural obstacles that prevent people from acting on the vision. This includes systems (performance management that rewards old behavior), managers who block change, and policies that contradict the new direction
- **Step 6 — Short-Term Wins**: Visible, unambiguous early successes that demonstrate the change is working. Wins must be planned, not hoped for. They build credibility for the larger change effort and silence critics
- **Step 7 — Consolidate Gains**: The most common failure point — declaring victory too early after early wins. Real change requires years, not months. Use the momentum from early wins to tackle bigger barriers
- **Step 8 — Anchor in Culture**: Change becomes lasting only when it is embedded in "how we do things here" — reflected in norms, stories, and organizational behaviors. Culture is the last thing to change, not the first
- **Kotter vs. ADKAR**: Kotter describes what leaders must do at the organizational level; ADKAR describes what individuals must go through. Kotter's Steps 1-4 create the conditions for ADKAR Awareness and Desire; Steps 5-8 support Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement

## Method Application
Method uses Kotter's framework to structure the change management track of transformation programs alongside delivery. The framework provides a diagnostic for programs experiencing adoption failure: which step was skipped or underinvested? Short-term wins (Step 6) are often explicitly designed and scheduled as delivery milestones — a pilot launch, a capability demo, an early user success story.

## Consulting Insight
🎯 **Consulting Tool — Kotter's 8-Step**: The most common failure mode is Step 7 — change programs that celebrate early wins as completion rather than evidence of momentum. Technology programs that go live and immediately withdraw the implementation team to start the next program often watch adoption erode over the following six months. The winning condition is not go-live; it's the new behavior becoming the default.
